How Hackable Is Your Speech Quality Metric?

A Corrected Protocol, a Benchmark, and What Patching Buys

Ali Alavi · Donald S. Williamson Department of Computer Science and Engineering, The Ohio State University

Speech quality predictors such as NISQA, DNSMOS and UTMOS are now used as training rewards, so it matters how much score an optimiser can extract without improving the audio. We fix two flaws in how that is measured, benchmark four widely used predictors under one protocol, and audit whether patching a predictor on its own hacks makes it a safer reward.

Abstract

Speech quality predictors are increasingly used as rewards, yet no agreed measure of their hackability exists. The usual measurement has two flaws. First, the perturbation reaches the predictor through a processing chain (here a neural codec) that shifts the score on its own, which scoring against the raw input charges to the attack. Referencing the unperturbed round trip instead changes measured hackability by up to a factor of four (0.31 to 0.08 for one defence). Second, one trained attacker is a sample, not a measurement: five attackers differing only in random seed reach success rates from 0.00 to 0.38 against one fixed predictor, so a defence claim needs the worst case over several. Under this protocol, four published predictors differ widely: NISQA is hacked on 90% of utterances, SSL-MOS on 21%, DNSMOS on 14% and UTMOS on 6%. We then audit a closed attack–detect–patch loop. It hardens the predictor only in its own attack space, by less than the spread between attackers; a random-perturbation baseline matches it; and it costs up to 0.30 system SRCC out of domain. Enhancers post-trained against patched predictors hack them far less (PESQ −0.03 versus −0.23). Code, preregistration and run outputs are released.

Two corrections to the usual measurement

Attacks on quality predictors are usually scored by comparing the attacked audio with the original recording, and reported for one trained attacker. Both choices change the answer.

Reference the codec round trip

Naive R(y) − R(x) > τ
Corrected R(y) − R(x̂) > τ

The perturbation reaches the predictor through a neural codec, and the codec moves the score on its own: by −0.10 MOS for SSL-MOS, −0.07 for UTMOS, +0.02 for DNSMOS and +0.14 for NISQA, and by up to +0.95 for a patched predictor. Scoring against the raw input x charges that offset to the attack. Scoring against the zero-perturbation round trip x̂ moves the multi-space defence from apparently catastrophic (0.306) to among the most robust (0.082).

Report the worst of several attackers

An attacker is itself a trained model. Five attackers that differ only in random seed reach success rates from 0.000 to 0.261 against the undefended predictor R0 and from 0.000 to 0.382 against the patched R3, a spread as large as every defence effect we measure.

Between predictors the spread is small relative to the differences (NISQA 0.876–0.916 against UTMOS 0.042–0.076), so the benchmark ranking holds where defence comparisons do not. We report the worst of five attackers.

Attacker, detector and patch loop

A policy trained with group relative policy optimisation (GRPO) adds one offset to the codec latents of each utterance, rescaled to a fixed size ε. It sees only the predictor's scores, so any predictor can be a target. An output counts as a hack only if its score rises and at least one independent measure (ESTOI, SI-SDR, SpeechBERTScore or the Whisper word error rate) says it got worse. No human labels are used. Each hack becomes a preference pair x̂ ≻ y, and the predictor is fine-tuned on its regression loss plus a margin ranking loss over every hack found so far.

Listen to the attacks

Each attacked clip is paired with the reference its score is measured from: the codec round trip for latent attacks, the clean input for waveform PGD. Switch between the two while a clip plays to hear what the attack changed; most perturbations are small, so headphones help. A clip counts as hacked when the attacked predictor scores it more than τ = 0.3 above the reference and at least one panel measure says it got worse.

The clips are not from the paper’s test set, because BVCC may not be redistributed. The released attackers, trained on BVCC, are applied unchanged to VoiceBank-DEMAND test speech, and every score is computed on the exact file you hear.

Common predictors differ by more than an order of magnitude

At one perturbation size NISQA is hacked on 89.6% of utterances for a mean gain of +0.954 MOS, SSL-MOS on 20.5%, DNSMOS OVRL on 13.6% and UTMOS on 5.7%. NISQA and DNSMOS are routinely used as reinforcement-learning rewards, so the choice of reward is a first-order robustness decision, and the ranking is not predictable from in-domain accuracy.

What patching buys

We start from SSL-MOS trained on BVCC (R0) and run three rounds of the loop (R1 to R3), a multi-space variant that also attacks DAC latents and the waveform, and three baselines that patch on noise, waveform-PGD or random latent pairs with the same loss and step budget.

  • It hardens only its own attack space

    EnCodec attack success falls from 0.226 (R0) to 0.050 (R1) and 0.017 (R2), but the R0→R3 difference does not hold its sign across seeds (−0.073, −0.039, +0.379).

  • A random baseline matches it

    Pairs from random latent perturbations of the same size, with no reinforcement learning and no detector, give the lowest in-space success rate of any defence (0.019 ± 0.016).

  • Nothing transfers

    Against an unseen DAC attacker the EnCodec-patched predictors are worse than undefended (0.065 rising to between 0.13 and 0.16), and white-box PGD succeeds on nearly every utterance. Only multi-space training resists DAC (0.016), and it is the one predictor PGD does not fully break (0.841).

  • Robustness costs agreement with listeners

    Out of domain, mean system SRCC falls from 0.731 to 0.690, 0.682 and 0.637 over three rounds and to 0.552 for the multi-space predictor; in domain the ranking holds (0.920 vs 0.922) but utterance MSE rises from 0.234 to 0.272.

Downstream, patched predictors are safer rewards

A small speech enhancer, pre-trained on VoiceBank-DEMAND, is post-trained with GRPO using one predictor's score as its only reward. Against R0 it shows the textbook hack: the reward rises by +0.263 while PESQ falls by 0.226, ESTOI by 0.057 and SI-SDR by 6.1 dB. Against R3 the reward rises as much (+0.269) with PESQ intact (−0.034), and the random-pair baseline again matches it.

Limitations

  • The attacker is black-box and mild. Its hacks against SSL-MOS move ESTOI by −0.023 and SI-SDR by −1.034 dB, and two independent judges call them perceptually marginal (UTMOS −0.004, DNSMOS −0.048; PGD moves UTMOS by +0.802). The benchmark ranks predictors against this attack family only.
  • We ran no listening test.
  • The detector is not fully independent: two panel measures are neural, and for waveform attacks its reference is the input itself, so success rates are not comparable across attack families.
  • The patch study uses 3 seeds and one predictor family (46 A100-hours in total).

Reproduce every number

The release holds every checkpoint behind the paper (195 files, 19.3 GB: predictors, every patch round, all attackers and enhancers), the exact configuration of each run, the per-run outputs and the preregistration. Each table, figure and result has its own notebook. A notebook either re-runs the evaluation from the released checkpoints on a GPU, or aggregates the released run outputs on a CPU in seconds, and then compares its numbers with the paper's.

# environment (Python 3.11)
git clone https://github.com/saliAlavi/audio_assessment_hackability
cd audio_assessment_hackability
pip install -r requirements.txt
pip install --no-deps -r requirements-nodeps.txt
export SCRATCH_DIR=/path/with/space   # checkpoints, caches, runs
source scripts/env.sh

# every number in the paper from the released outputs (CPU)
NB_SOURCE=released jupyter nbconvert --to notebook --execute \
  notebooks/results/paper_numbers.ipynb

# or re-run the evaluations from checkpoints (77 jobs, ~17 A100-hours)
NB_SOURCE=recompute NB_RUN=local jupyter nbconvert --to notebook \
  --execute notebooks/results/paper_numbers.ipynb
